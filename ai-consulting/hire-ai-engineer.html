Hire an AI engineer or bring in help: what is the real choice?

The choice: hire an AI engineer, bring in outside help, or both, and in what order.14 Few firms start with a hire; training staff and new workflows are far more common.1,2 What makes it work is a named workflow, a measure and an owner, not the hire.16,17

What should you walk away with?

You should leave with one priced workflow, a before-and-after measure, a working pilot with a go-or-stop date, a job spec if needed, and a written handover.16,19

  • One workflow, picked and priced: what it costs today in hours, errors or cash.16,19 RAND's most common root cause of failure is a misunderstood problem.16
  • A before-and-after measure, set before anyone builds.19 RAND sees models tuned to the wrong metrics; Google's second rule is to design metrics first.16,19
  • A working pilot in your own systems, with a go-or-stop date about 90 days out.15 Mid-market top performers took about 90 days from pilot to full use, in a preliminary, self-reported study.15
  • A job spec for your first AI hire, if the pilot shows you need one.14 Ng's playbook builds the in-house team after pilots; one IT leader's advice is to start with a lead.14
  • A handover written into the contract: runbooks, test data and who owns the system after launch.17

Where does your team stay in charge?

Your people keep the decisions: the ops lead owns the measure, the COO signs off on go-live, front-line staff check output, and a named owner runs it afterward.17,18

  • Ops lead or process owner: owns the problem and the success measure.17 RAND's top fix is to make sure technical staff understand the purpose and the domain.16
  • COO or division head: signs off on go-live and owns the risk call.17 The AI playbook from NIST, the US standards body, puts AI risk decisions with executive leadership.17
  • Front-line staff: check AI output before it reaches a customer.18 Of firms using AI, 66% use it only to help people with tasks.1
  • A named internal owner: runs the system after handover, and the AI hire works toward that owner's goal.17

What shapes can the work take?

Prove one workflow before hiring if you have no engineers, bring in help if you have some IT, and train your team for everyday tasks.14,1

1. About 40 to 150 staff, no engineers: prove one workflow, hire later

One painful workflow, no engineers on staff and no AI in production.1 Ng's playbook starts with pilots that show traction before building a team.14

  1. COOPick the workflow and say what it costs today
  2. Outside advisorRank two or three AI bets and write specs
  3. Ops leadSet the before-and-after measure
  4. Division headGive the builder one subject-matter expert for the pilot
  5. IT partner or vendorBuild the pilot from the specs
  6. Front-line staffCheck every AI output during the pilot
  7. COOGo, change or stop at about day 90

2. About 150 to 500 staff, some IT: outside help now, a lead alongside

A second and third use case are queued, and an IT or data person can host the work. Partner-led pilots reached deployment about twice as often as internal builds; the study calls that correlation, not cause.15

  1. COOApprove the role, the budget and the first workflow
  2. Outside advisorRun the first workflow and write the AI lead's job spec
  3. HR leadRecruit, planning for 39 to 75 days with the seat empty
  4. New AI leadBuild with the advisor, then own the system
  5. Ops leadOwn the measure, and roll out at one site or team first
  6. ControllerTrack spend against the measured gain

Cost: The hire runs about $205,000 to $275,000 a year with benefits, by our math from BLS pay data.5,7 Zavient advisory can run alongside, but it is not like for like, since advice is not engineering.25

3. Any size, everyday tasks: make the team you have better

The gain is staff using AI tools in daily work, not a custom system.1 Lightcast found 51% of job ads asking for AI skills are outside IT.13

  1. Division headName three to five tasks per team to try
  2. Team leadsTrain staff and set rules on what data goes in
  3. StaffUse the tools and log time saved and errors
  4. Ops leadReview the log monthly and stop what doesn't help

Cost: Staff time and tool licences, with no new hire. By our math, training current staff is more than ten times as common as hiring AI-trained staff.1,2

Why do most AI pilots stall?

Most pilots stall because nobody pinned down the problem, the data isn't ready, or no one owns the system after the build.16,20

  • Hiring before picking the problem: RAND's most common root cause is a misunderstood or miscommunicated problem.16 Fix: name the workflow and its measure before you write a job ad.19
  • One hire, no usable data: Gartner expects 60% of AI projects without AI-ready data to be abandoned through 2026.20,16 Fix: check data access on a real sample and name a business owner first.17
  • The empty seat: technical roles take a median 75 days to first fill, against 39 days for nonexecutive roles overall.10,8 Fix: start the first workflow with outside help while you recruit.
  • Outside help that never hands over. Fix: write runbooks, test data and training into the contract as milestones.17
  • Pilots that stall: Gartner predicted 30% of generative AI projects would be dropped after a trial build (proof of concept) by end of 2025.21 Fix: set a go-or-stop date and a dollar measure at the start.15
  • A lone engineer with no domain help: RAND found data scientists rely on subject-matter experts to know what the data means.16 Fix: pair the hire with the ops lead from day one, so the team shapes the work.

How do you judge any AI advisor?

Judge an advisor on your own numbers: record today's baseline, test on real cases they never saw, price the lift, and get the owner and go-or-stop date in writing.19,7

  1. Write down today's numbers for one workflow: time per item, error rate and monthly cost.19
  2. Ask the advisor or candidate to restate your problem in your words before naming any tool.16
  3. Ask whether the first version needs AI at all. Google's first rule: don't be afraid to launch without machine learning.19
  4. Hold back recent real cases the builder never sees, such as last month's, and test on those.
  5. Compare the AI-assisted output with your best person's work on the same cases. Count errors, not demos.
  6. Price the lift: hours saved times loaded cost per hour, minus the cost to run it. BLS puts benefits at 31.5% of pay costs for professional and management staff.7
  7. Ask who owns the system after day 90, and get the answer in writing.17
  8. Agree a go-or-stop date before the pilot starts. Mid-market top performers reached full use in about 90 days.15

Questions buyers ask about hiring an AI engineer

How much does an AI engineer cost?

About $205,000 to $275,000 a year with benefits, by our math from BLS pay for the closest occupation at the median and 75th percentile.5,6,7 WTW says AI and machine learning engineers commonly get base pay premiums of 15% to 20%.12 Recruiting comes on top: SHRM's median is $1,200 a hire, and one AI recruiter illustrates agency fees of 20% to 25% of base.9,11

Should I hire full-time or use outside help?

Start with outside help on one workflow, and hire once you know what works.14 Few firms hire first: 1.3% of AI-using firms hired AI-trained staff.2,1

Consulting firm or an in-house AI team: which makes more sense financially?

Outside help makes more financial sense for a 40-500 person firm's first workflows; hire in-house once AI work fills a full-time role.14 One engineer costs about $205,000 to $275,000 a year with benefits, by our math from BLS pay data.5,7 Partner-led pilots also reached deployment about twice as often in MIT's study, a correlation, not proof.15

Who owns the AI system after outside help leaves?

Name an internal owner before work starts, and put knowledge transfer in the contract as milestones.17

How long does it take to hire an AI engineer?

Plan on about six to eleven weeks with the seat empty. SHRM's median for nonexecutive roles is 39 days; Ashby's for technical roles is 75 days.8,10

When should you not hire help?

Skip outside help when the gain is everyday tool use, when three or more queued use cases justify an AI lead, or when a packaged product does the job.1,14,22

  • The gain is everyday tool use, not a custom system: train the staff you have instead (shape 3).1 By our math, training is more than ten times as common as hiring for AI.2
  • Three or more use cases are queued and your data sits in one place: hire an AI lead now.14 Use outside help only to scope the first quarter.
  • A packaged product already does the job: buy it. Menlo Ventures, an investor, found 76% of enterprise AI use cases are bought, not built.22
  • You need free help to start: try a Small Business Development Center, or a NIST MEP center if you make things.23,24
