What is AI governance consulting, and what does it change?

AI governance consulting builds your AI governance framework, so staff know who owns AI, which tools and data are allowed and who checks output.2,1 Done well, it speeds adoption.6,7 It succeeds on a fast approval route and evidence an auditor can read, not a long policy.6,8

What should you walk away with?

You should leave with a named AI owner, a register of every AI tool, an approved-tools list with data rules, risk tiers and an evidence file.2,3,9

  • You should leave with five things: a named owner, a register of AI tools, approved tools with data rules, risk tiers and an evidence file.2,9
  • A named owner, usually the COO (chief operating officer), with sign-off from the owner or board. Firms where the C-suite owns AI report a return far more often (62% vs 18%).2,6
  • A register: one list of every AI tool and use, including AI built into vendor software. NIST (the US standards agency) starts governance with this inventory.2,31
  • An approved-tools list, data rules for what may go into each tool, and a request route that answers in days. Only 27% of smaller firms have data rules today.3,6
  • Risk tiers: drafting an email is low risk; decisions on credit, claims or hiring are high and get a human checkpoint.44,43
  • An evidence file an examiner or auditor can read: training records, reviews and incidents, refreshed each quarter. Most insurers say controls exist but evidence is scattered (68%).9,20

Where does your team stay in charge?

Your COO owns the AI register, and a named lending, claims or HR lead signs off any AI that touches a decision about customers or staff.2,25,26

  • Your people keep ownership, risk calls, customer and HR decisions, and the final check on every output; AI decides none of them.2,25
  • The COO owns the register and the quarterly review. The head of compliance or risk sets risk tiers and keeps the evidence file.2,17
  • A lending, underwriting or claims lead signs off any AI that touches a customer decision. California's rules define real review: the reviewer understands the output and can change it.25,12
  • The HR lead owns AI in hiring, discipline and firing. California's SB 947 bars relying on software alone to discipline or fire a worker.26,24
  • Every user checks AI output before it leaves the building. Two in three workers say they rely on AI output without checking it.11,30

What shapes can the work take?

Smaller firms need a written policy and AI register first, banks and insurers need an examiner-ready build, and ISO 42001 only when customers ask.3,17,34

1. Policy and register (about 40 to 150 staff, no AI policy yet)

Pick this when staff already use public AI tools and nothing is written down. Six in ten smaller firms report exactly that.3

  1. Owner-CEOName the COO as AI owner
  2. COOList every AI tool in use, including features inside vendor software
  3. IT leadSet data rules for what may go into each tool
  4. Head of complianceWrite a short policy and an approved-tools list
  5. COOOpen a request route for new tools that answers in days
  6. Team leadPilot the approved tools in one team or site first
  7. COOTrain every user and keep the record

Cost: The NIST framework and playbook are free.1,2 The real cost is staff time: the COO, a compliance lead and IT, plus short training for every user.

2. Examiner-ready (banks, credit unions and insurers)

Pick this when an examiner or state insurance regulator will ask about your AI. The NAIC (the national body of state insurance regulators) holds senior management accountable to the board.17,21

  1. Head of complianceSort each AI use into a risk tier
  2. Chief lending officer or claims leadSet the human checkpoint in each high-tier workflow
  3. Vendor managerSend due-diligence questions to each AI vendor
  4. Model risk leadValidate before first use, with someone who didn't build the model
  5. Head of complianceBuild the evidence file
  6. COOReport to the board each quarter

Cost: No neutral published price exists for this work, so judge it by its outputs. Expect risk tiers, vendor reviews, validation notes and an evidence file your examiner can read.20,21

3. Certification track (customers or partners ask for ISO 42001)

Pick this only when customers or partners ask for proof. ISO/IEC 42001 certification is voluntary, and accredited bodies certify, not ISO itself.34

  1. COOConfirm a customer actually requires the certificate
  2. Head of complianceBuy the standard (CHF 225) and build the management system on your NIST map35
  3. Internal auditorRun an internal audit before the external one
  4. Certification bodyRun the two-stage external audit
  5. COOBudget a yearly check-up (surveillance) audit, which one vendor puts at 30% to 40% of the first fee36

Cost: One compliance software vendor puts first-year cost at $15,000 to $40,000 for a small firm and $40,000 to $90,000 mid-size.36 Another vendor says $20,000 to $80,000 for 50 to 250 staff (both read 26 Nov 2026).37

Why do most AI pilots stall?

AI pilots stall when governance is too slow or too strict: bans push use underground, and firms approving tools in days report better returns than those taking months.10,6

  • Most stall because the rules don't fit real work: bans push AI underground, policies go unenforced, and vendor AI goes unchecked.10,4 Each fix is below.
  • A ban drives AI use underground. Among employees, misuse ran at 67% where AI was banned, against 33% with no policy, so approve safe tools instead.10
  • A policy nobody enforces. Only 34% of firms with a policy audit for unapproved AI, so check the register against real use each quarter.4
  • A policy so strict people ignore it. Of firms with an AI policy, 54% of HR leaders call it too restrictive; give low-risk uses a fast approval.38,6
  • Shadow AI (tools used without approval) leaks data: one in five breached firms in IBM's study had such a breach. Heavy shadow AI added $670,000 per breach, so give staff an approved tool.4
  • Vendor AI nobody checked. The FTC banned Rite Aid from facial recognition for five years after undocumented vendor checks, so write down every vendor review.39
  • AI agents acting with no sign-off: 47% of senior AI executives in an EY survey said urgent deployments skipped governance.40,41 Require a named approver before any agent acts alone.41

How do you judge any AI advisor?

Judge any AI advisor on whether they deliver a register and an evidence file, not just a policy, plus a tool approval route that answers in days.8,9,6

  1. Judge them on what they leave you, not the policy they write: a register, an evidence file, named owners and a fast approval route.8,2
  2. Ask for a register and an evidence file, not just a policy. Evidence is the gap: most business leaders lack strong confidence they'd pass an audit.8,9
  3. Ask which rules apply to you, by state and regulator, as of this month. Compare the answer with our table above.16,15
  4. Ask how fast staff can get a new tool approved under their design. Days beat months on returns.6
  5. Ask who owns each control after the advisor leaves. NIST asks for named roles and executive responsibility.2
  6. Ask whether they map to NIST first and add ISO 42001 only if customers need it. Certification is voluntary.1,34
  7. Ask how they cover AI built into software you already buy. FINRA and the NCUA both expect it reviewed.31,21
  8. Ask whether the policy mostly bans AI or lets people use it safely. Bans push use underground.10,38
  9. Record today's numbers first: tools in use, days to approve a new tool, and open AI incidents. Check them again after 90 days.
  10. Ask for a fixed scope with named deliverables, so you can check what you got.

Questions buyers ask about AI governance consulting

Does a firm our size need an AI governance framework?

Yes, if staff use AI, and 6 in 10 small and mid-market business owners say their staff already do.3 Colorado's rewrite dropped its under-50-staff exemption, and insurer and credit union regulators expect oversight at any size.13,17,21

Who usually owns AI governance in a company?

At 40 to 500 staff, usually the COO, with the owner or board signing off. NIST asks for named roles and executive responsibility.2 Firms with C-suite ownership report a return far more often.6,45

Should we block AI tools or approve some?

Approve some, because employees reported risky AI use far more where AI was banned (67%) than where there was no policy (33%).10 Approve a few safe tools and set clear data rules.3

Does following NIST or ISO 42001 make us compliant?

No: a framework is a good map, but Colorado dropped its NIST and ISO safe harbor in the 2026 rewrite.13 Each rule adds its own duties, such as New York City's bias audit for hiring tools.23

Does SR 11-7 still apply to AI at our bank?

No: SR 26-2 replaced it on 17 Apr 2026, aimed mostly at banks over $30 billion in assets.18 The OCC's matching bulletin covers community banks within limits and leaves generative AI out of scope.19 It still expects validation before first use and challenge by people who didn't build the model.20

Will federal law override state AI laws?

Not yet: a December 2025 executive order set up a Justice Department task force, but it voids no state law by itself.32 Congress has passed no preemption law, so plan for the state rules.33

When should you not hire help?

Skip outside help when a compliance lead has time to use the free NIST playbook, your question is purely legal, or you only need the ISO 42001 certificate.2,12,34

  • When you have a compliance lead with time: do it in-house with the free NIST AI RMF Playbook.2
  • You already track ISO 27001 or SOC 2 (a System and Organization Controls audit) in compliance software. Add AI governance there, in tools such as Compyl or Scytale.36,37
  • The question is purely legal, like a Colorado notice or a New York City bias audit: ask your law firm.12,23
  • You only need the ISO 42001 certificate: go straight to an accredited certification body.34
