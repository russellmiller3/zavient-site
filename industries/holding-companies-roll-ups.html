Roll-up strategy: what do holdcos actually use AI for after close?

Bottom line
  • Roll-ups win on operations: growth and margin, not resale multiples, drive returns for buy-and-build deals and serial acquirers.1,2
  • Acquired companies usually start with little AI: 43% of private equity (PE) portfolio companies make no real use of it.3,4
  • Start with shared finance and clean data, then pricing and cross-sell; front-line automation comes last.5,4
  • Keep acquired owners and staff: roll-ups did better when founders stayed, and acquired workers leave fast.6,7
  • AI roll-ups have raised billions, but their results are still unproven and mostly self-reported.8,9

What is a roll-up strategy, and what does AI change in it?

A roll-up strategy buys several small firms in one fragmented industry and runs them as one larger business.1,6 AI changes the operating work across those firms: shared finance, clean data, pricing and cross-sell.5,3 Done with the acquired teams, it drives the growth that separates winning roll-ups.1,6

Where does your team stay in charge?

Your people keep every decision: the holdco CEO owns the AI plan, controllers sign off each close, general managers own pricing, and people decide hiring and pay.4,10,12

  • The holdco CEO owns the AI plan and its budget. In a Latin American (LATAM) survey of 10 operators, CEO-led AI work was judged worth it 4 times in 5, against 1 in 5 otherwise.4
  • Each company's controller signs off its close and every intercompany entry, while AI drafts matches and flags differences. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) AI framework asks firms to name who oversees each AI system.10
  • Each company's general manager (GM) owns pricing and customer changes, and acquired founders sit on the rollout group. Roll-ups did better when acquired owners stayed on as directors and shareholders.6,11
  • People make hiring, pay and discipline calls, with a documented review. Colorado's 2026 law gives people a right to request human review of adverse automated decisions, including in employment.12

Where does AI pay first in a holdco or roll-up?

AI pays first in shared finance and clean data across companies, then in pricing and cross-sell, then in integrating each new add-on.5,4,1

  1. Shared finance: the group close, consolidation and intercompany entries

    Close to half of add-ons are a platform's fourth deal or later, so each month-end spans more ledgers.1 Only 14% of portfolio finance functions have moved past back-office automation, operating partners say.5

    Payoff to measure: Days to close the group books, and intercompany differences cleared before month-end.

  2. Data standards: one chart of accounts, customer list and customer relationship management (CRM) system

    Operating partners rank data infrastructure as the top barrier to AI.5 In a LATAM survey of 10 operators, 7 in 10 acquired companies had no CRM.4

    Payoff to measure: Share of companies reporting on the same chart of accounts and customer fields.

  3. Pricing, cross-sell and the sales pipeline across companies

    Buy-and-build deals that also grew returned 2.2x, against 1.4x from reselling at a higher multiple alone.1 PE leaders aim AI at revenue more than cost: 41% against 24%.3

    Payoff to measure: Revenue from customers sold a second company's service, and win rate on quotes.

  4. Integrating each new add-on

    Integrating AI into the next acquired business is not much easier than into the last one.13 AI roll-up investors name integration and change management as the top risk, at 79%.14

    Payoff to measure: Days from close until the add-on reports in the group's format.

  5. Value creation plan and board reporting

    44% of operating partners report AI already drafting board packages in portfolio finance teams.5 Only 29% put AI's impact into board and investor reporting.5

    Payoff to measure: Hours to produce the monthly board pack, and plan items with a named owner and measure.

  6. Deal sourcing and diligence

    79% of independent sponsor professionals expect AI to significantly change their model.15 In 161 buy-and-build buyouts, buying add-ons at lower multiples than the platform explained much of the outperformance.16

    Payoff to measure: Targets screened per month, and days from first look to letter of intent.

  7. Training and onboarding across companies

    In the LATAM survey of 10 operators, 7 had no AI training program.4 Half named onboarding and marketing as a bottleneck.4

    Payoff to measure: Share of staff at each company using the shared tools every week.

  8. Front-line service work: tickets, tax returns, scheduling

    Ranked last because the evidence is company-reported: Current says its tools save 31% of time across 7,000 returns.17 Sloppy AI output can eat the gains in services work.18

    Payoff to measure: Time per ticket or return, with a reviewer checking a weekly sample.

Do roll-ups and serial acquirers create value?

Only when they run the companies better: buy-and-builds that relied on resale multiples returned 1.4x, against 2.2x for deals that also grew or lifted margins.1 Roll-up initial public offerings (IPOs) of the 1990s lost money on average.22,6

Where does AI go wrong in a holdco or roll-up?

AI goes wrong in a holdco when key people leave, integrations pile up, results are taken on trust, or customer data is pooled carelessly.7,13,8,28

  • Losing key people: 33% of acquired tech workers left in year one, against 12% of comparable hires.7 Losses run highest in executive, technical and sales roles, so give those people the new tools first.33
  • Treating each add-on as easy: announcement returns fall from the fifth deal on, and AI gets little easier per company.25,13 Give every integration its own owner and checklist.
  • Rolling out with no playbook: 41% of operating partners deploy AI across companies without one.5 Prove one playbook at one company, then copy it.
  • Paying software prices for services firms: automated outsourcing firms trade at 5-23 times operating profit (EBITDA), software at 22-92, per a Fortune commentary.34 Price deals on today's cash flow, not promised AI margins.9
  • Accepting sloppy output: low-quality AI work can wipe out the time saved in services.18 Sample output weekly at each company, with a named person signing off.
  • Pooling customer data carelessly: privacy promises travel with each acquired company.28 Map each company's notice before you merge customer lists or train AI on them.

Questions holdco operators and sponsors ask

What is a roll-up strategy?

A roll-up strategy buys several small companies in one fragmented industry and runs them as one larger business.1 It pays when the combined business grows or runs better, not only when it resells at a higher multiple.1,6

Do roll-ups work?

Only some do: 1990s roll-up IPOs lost money on average, while disciplined programmatic acquirers beat peers over a decade.22,2 The gap is operations, and keeping acquired owners involved.6,1

What is an AI roll-up?

An AI roll-up is a roll-up, usually venture-funded, that buys services firms and runs them on its own AI software.8,34 Targets include accounting, IT support and property management firms.17,21,19

Do AI roll-ups work?

Not proven yet: published results come from the companies or their backers, and investors question the economics.8,9 Our own check of nine public claims found none checked by an outsider.19,17

What is the difference between a serial acquirer and a roll-up?

A serial acquirer buys again and again, often keeping companies separate, while a roll-up usually merges them into one platform in one industry.23,1 Serial acquirers were involved in 68% of deals by public acquirers worldwide from 2000 to 2018.23

What is an independent sponsor?

An independent sponsor buys companies one deal at a time, raising equity for each deal instead of from a committed fund, often from family offices.15 In a 2026 survey of 187 sponsor professionals, 79% expect AI to significantly change the model.15

Should a holdco buy AI tools or build its own?

Buy standard tools for the close, CRM and ticketing, and build only the thin shared layer, such as one data model and group reporting.35,36

Buy: Buy tools for the jobs every company shares, such as the close, CRM and ticketing.35 Most enterprise AI is now bought: 76% of use cases in 2025, up from 53%.35 In MIT's study, as Fortune reported, bought tools and partnerships succeeded about 67% of the time, far more often than internal builds.36

Build: Build only the thin shared layer, such as one data model and group reporting, once several companies use it.13 AI roll-ups build whole platforms because the platform is their bet, funded by investors like General Catalyst and Thrive Holdings.19,21 A 40-500 person holdco rarely needs that.

When should you pick something else?

Pick something else when you own one company rather than a group, an ERP move is coming anyway, or staff only need faster writing.3,5,35

  • You own one PE-backed company, not a group: the cross-company jobs here don't apply yet. Start with that company's first AI job; 43% of portfolio companies make no real use of AI yet.3
  • An enterprise resource planning (ERP) move is coming at your companies anyway: finish it first, since data infrastructure is the top barrier to AI.5 Use the AI features in the new system before adding tools.
  • Your teams only need to write and summarise faster: an enterprise plan of ChatGPT, Claude or Microsoft 365 Copilot will do.35
  • You are still buying your first company: AI for screening and diligence matters more now. 79% of independent sponsor professionals expect AI to change their model.15
